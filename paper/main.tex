\title{A Study of Edge Orientation in Graph Neural Networks for Link Prediction}
\author{Nikita Scheglov\\Advisor: Victor Kitov, PhD}
\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
This study examines how retaining edge orientation during message passing affects directed link prediction with graph neural networks. We compare a feature-only multilayer perceptron, a symmetrized mean-aggregation encoder and an encoder with separate incoming and outgoing aggregation, using the same order-sensitive decoder architecture and a further symmetrized control with a matched parameter budget. The synthetic experiment uses 180 vertices with three latent roles and noisy role-related features, while varying directional structure over five levels and retaining the same undirected skeleton and features within each of five random seeds. Unordered vertex pairs are separated into training, validation and test groups, and both orientations of each training query are masked before message passing to prevent direct edge information from entering its prediction. Across 100 model runs, checkpoints are selected by validation average precision and evaluated using test ROC-AUC and average precision under a fixed one-to-one negative sampling ratio. At maximum directional strength, the directed encoder achieves ROC-AUC 0.723 compared with 0.575 for the capacity control, with a paired mean difference of 0.148 across seeds. The advantage is absent at weak directional structure, indicating that orientation-aware aggregation is useful under specific properties of this generating family rather than uniformly beneficial, and motivating subsequent evaluation on real directed networks.
\end{abstract}
\textbf{Keywords:} directed graphs, graph neural networks, link prediction, edge orientation.

\section{Introduction}
Directed graphs describe relations in which exchanging the source and target changes the meaning of an edge, as in citation and hyperlink networks.
Link prediction on such graphs requires estimating the existence of an ordered edge rather than merely deciding whether two vertices are connected.
The objective of this study is to assess how retaining edge orientation during neighborhood aggregation affects this prediction task.

GraphSAGE provides a simple inductive aggregation mechanism~\cite{sage}, while Dir-GNN explicitly separates incoming and outgoing neighborhoods~\cite{dirgnn}.
Spectral approaches such as MagNet encode direction through complex-valued operators~\cite{magnet}.
For link prediction, gravity-inspired and directed graph autoencoders additionally introduce asymmetric scoring functions~\cite{gravity,digae}.
These studies establish relevant encoder and decoder designs, but the contribution of each component must be distinguished in a comparison.

An asymmetric decoder can assign different scores to reversed pairs even when its input embeddings were obtained from a symmetrized graph.
Conversely, a symmetric inner-product decoder cannot express this distinction solely because its encoder preserves orientation~\cite{vgae,complex}.
Moreover, reported prediction quality depends on data splits, negative sampling and baseline tuning~\cite{heart}.
The present study therefore focuses on an encoder comparison with a common order-sensitive decoder and a shared evaluation protocol.

The working hypothesis is that separate incoming and outgoing aggregation improves link prediction when direction carries information about vertex roles.
The study compares a feature-only model, a symmetrized mean-aggregation encoder and a directed encoder, and examine how their quality changes as directional structure varies.
The contribution sought is a controlled characterization of this dependence; the study does not propose a new message-passing architecture.

The first experiment uses synthetic static graphs with fixed size and expected density and adjustable directional structure.
The compared models receive identical vertex features, data splits and sampled negative pairs for each random seed.
ROC-AUC and average precision measure prediction quality, with validation used for model selection and the test set reserved for final evaluation.
This experiment is intended to identify the conditions under which preserving orientation is useful before extending the comparison to an open real-world graph.

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Message passing.}
Message-passing networks separate message construction, neighborhood aggregation and readout~\cite{mpnn}.
GCN, GraphSAGE and GAT provide standard aggregation mechanisms~\cite{gcn,sage,gat}, while GIN and GATv2 clarify the expressive limitations of aggregation and attention~\cite{gin,gatv2}.
Mean aggregation offers a simple reference for comparing a shared undirected neighborhood with separate incoming and outgoing neighborhoods.

\paragraph{Directed representations.}
Dir-GNN separates incoming and outgoing messages and studies their benefit for node classification~\cite{dirgnn}.
DGCN and DiGCN incorporate directed proximity and diffusion~\cite{dgcn,digcn}, whereas DEDGAT and ADPA distinguish directional roles and patterns~\cite{dedgat,adpa}.
These classification results motivate a directed encoder but do not establish an improvement in link prediction.
MagNet encodes orientation through a magnetic Laplacian~\cite{magnet}; MSGNN and SigMaNet extend spectral modeling to signed directed graphs~\cite{msgnn,sigmanet}.
DUPLEX combines directional attention and complex embeddings~\cite{duplex}.
DiGCL and S2-DiGCL introduce contrastive learning for directed representations~\cite{digcl,s2digcl}.
Directed positional encodings provide another route to retaining directional structure~\cite{digtransformer,multiq}.

\paragraph{Asymmetric decoding.}
The standard inner-product decoder of a variational graph autoencoder assigns identical scores to reversed pairs~\cite{vgae}.
Gravity-inspired autoencoders introduce an asymmetric decoder~\cite{gravity}, while directed graph autoencoders learn source and target representations~\cite{digae}.
LINE and node2vec provide alternative network-embedding references~\cite{line,node2vec}.
For a fixed relation, DistMult uses a symmetric score; ComplEx can represent asymmetric relations~\cite{distmult,complex}.
Consequently, directed message passing and asymmetric edge scoring are distinct design choices.

\paragraph{Pairwise structure.}
SEAL predicts links from enclosing subgraphs, whereas NBFNet learns query-dependent path representations~\cite{seal,nbfnet}.
Neo-GNNs and Neural Common Neighbor exploit neighborhood overlap~\cite{neognn,ncn}; ELPH and BUDDY approximate link-specific structure through sketching~\cite{buddy}.
TNCN extends common-neighbor representations to temporal graphs~\cite{tncn}.
These approaches motivate structural controls, but introduce additional mechanisms beyond changing edge orientation in the encoder.

\paragraph{Data and evaluation.}
H2GCN and LINKX study learning beyond homophily~\cite{h2gcn,linkx}; heterophily and edge directionality should be treated as separate properties.
Platonov et al. identify dataset weaknesses and leakage in heterophily evaluation~\cite{critical}.
OGB supplies task-specific splits and evaluation protocols~\cite{ogb}, and HeaRT highlights the sensitivity of link prediction to tuning, splits and negative sampling~\cite{heart}.
Dynamic link-prediction protocols and TGB benchmarks further demonstrate the importance of matching evaluation to the prediction task~\cite{dynamic_eval,tgb,tgb2}.
The initial study therefore considers static directed edge recovery with shared splits and negative examples; temporal and multi-relational prediction are separate extensions.

\paragraph{Scope of the comparison.}
The study focuses on a controlled comparison of directional neighborhood aggregation.
A common order-sensitive decoder is used with feature-only, symmetrized and directed encoders.
The synthetic experiment varies directional structure while keeping graph size and expected density fixed, with prediction quality summarized across random seeds.
This design separates the contribution of the encoder from decoder asymmetry and evaluation choices.

\section{Directed edge recovery}
\label{sec:problem}
Given a partially observed directed graph and vertex features, the task is to predict whether an ordered pair of distinct vertices forms an edge.
The initial setting is transductive: all vertices are available during training, but validation and test edges are withheld.
Graphs are static, unweighted and contain a single edge type; self-loops are excluded from the prediction task.

\subsection{Data and notation}
Let $V=\{1,\ldots,n\}$ and let $\mathcal U=\{(u,v)\in V^2:u\ne v\}$ be the candidate ordered pairs.
The full synthetic graph is $G=(V,E)$, where $E\subseteq\mathcal U$, and $X\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}$ contains vertex features.
The target is $y_{uv}=\mathbf 1\{(u,v)\in E\}$.
In particular, $y_{uv}$ and $y_{vu}$ need not coincide; a reversed edge is not automatically a negative example.
For synthetic graphs, absent pairs have known labels under the specified generator.

Synthetic instances are sampled from a family $P_\rho$ that varies directional structure while keeping $n$ and the undirected skeleton fixed across $\rho$.
The generating distribution is specified in Section~\ref{sec:experiment}.
The initial comparison assumes that these roles influence connectivity and that vertex features provide noisy information about them.
For each $\rho$, graph instances, splits and initialization seeds are recorded.
Conclusions from this setting are restricted to the specified generating family.

\subsection{Training graph and labeled pairs}
Unordered vertex pairs $\{u,v\}$ are randomly partitioned into training, validation and test groups with proportions $0.8$, $0.1$ and $0.1$.
Both orientations of a pair belong to the same group.
Positive sets $E_s$ contain the edges in group $s\in\{\mathrm{tr},\mathrm{va},\mathrm{te}\}$.
Negative sets $N_s$ are sampled uniformly without replacement from absent ordered pairs in that group, with $|N_s|=|E_s|$.
Thus positive and negative examples are disjoint across splits and reciprocal positive edges cannot occur in different splits.
Each labeled sample is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal D_s=\{(u,v,1):(u,v)\in E_s\}
\cup\{(u,v,0):(u,v)\in N_s\}.
\end{equation}
The message-passing adjacency satisfies $(A_{\mathrm{tr}})_{uv}=\mathbf 1\{(u,v)\in E_{\mathrm{tr}}\}$.
Validation and test labels are never supplied to the encoder, including through symmetrization or feature construction.
For each training batch, both orientations of every query pair are removed from the message-passing graph before computing the loss, including reverse edges of negative queries.
At validation and test time the encoder uses the fixed training graph only.

\subsection{Model class}
For $m\in\mathcal M=\{\mathrm{MLP},\mathrm{Sym},\mathrm{Dir}\}$ and an adjacency matrix $A$, the encoder produces
$H=f^{(m)}_\phi(X,A)\in\mathbb R^{n\times h}$.
The neighborhoods below refer to $A_{\mathrm{tr}}$; for a masked adjacency they are formed from the remaining edges.
The feature-only model processes each $x_v$ independently.
The symmetrized encoder aggregates over
$\mathcal N_{\mathrm{sym}}(v)=\{u:(u,v)\in E_{\mathrm{tr}}\text{ or }(v,u)\in E_{\mathrm{tr}}\}$.
The directed encoder aggregates incoming and outgoing neighborhoods separately:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal N_{\mathrm{in}}(v)&=\{u:(u,v)\in E_{\mathrm{tr}}\},\\
\mathcal N_{\mathrm{out}}(v)&=\{u:(v,u)\in E_{\mathrm{tr}}\}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Mean aggregation over an empty neighborhood is defined as the zero vector.
Both graph encoders retain a separate self-feature transformation, use the same number of layers and are derived from the GraphSAGE and Dir-GNN designs~\cite{sage,dirgnn}.
Their widths and parameter counts are reported, with an additional parameter-budget control to assess the effect of model capacity.

All encoders use the same decoder architecture and input ordering:
\begin{equation}
p_\theta^{(m)}(u,v;A)=\sigma\!\left(g_\psi([h_u\Vert h_v])\right),
\qquad \sigma(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Here $[h_u\Vert h_v]$ denotes concatenation and $g_\psi$ is a multilayer perceptron with a scalar output.
Its parameters are learned separately for each model; the architecture is shared, not the fitted weights.
The decoder permits $p_\theta^{(m)}(u,v;A)\ne p_\theta^{(m)}(v,u;A)$, without requiring this inequality for every pair.

\subsection{Quality criteria and model selection}
The internal criterion is mean binary cross-entropy with $L_2$ regularization, where $\lambda>0$ is fixed within a comparison.
The encoder parameters and decoder parameters are optimized jointly.
Let $\mathfrak B$ be a recorded partition of $\mathcal D_{\mathrm{tr}}$ into batches.
For each batch $B$, let $A_{\mathrm{tr}}^{(-B)}$ be the training adjacency with both orientations of every query pair removed, regardless of its label.
The batch-specific probability and binary cross-entropy are
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
q_{\theta,B}^{(m)}(u,v)&=p_\theta^{(m)}(u,v;A_{\mathrm{tr}}^{(-B)}),\\
\ell(y,p)&=-y\log p-(1-y)\log(1-p).
\end{split}
\end{equation}

The external criteria are ROC-AUC and average precision (AP), computed on the same labeled pairs for all models.
AP is the sum of precision values at successive score thresholds weighted by the corresponding increments in recall.
ROC-AUC is the probability that a positive example is ranked above a negative one, counting ties as one half.
The negative-to-positive ratio is fixed at $1:1$; AP values therefore describe this sampled evaluation distribution.
Hyperparameters are fixed in the initial experiment; checkpoints are selected by validation AP under a common maximum epoch count and early-stopping rule.
Test labels are used only for final evaluation.

For $K$ recorded random seeds, the comparison reports the mean and sample standard deviation of each test metric.
The directional effect is summarized by paired differences
\begin{equation}
\Delta_M(\rho)=\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K}
\left(M^{(k)}_{\mathrm{Dir}}(\rho)-M^{(k)}_{\mathrm{Sym}}(\rho)\right),
\qquad M\in\{\mathrm{ROC\mbox{-}AUC},\mathrm{AP}\},
\end{equation}
using the same graph instance, features, split and negative pairs within each seed.
A positive difference indicates improved performance under that configuration; an advantage is not assumed for every $\rho$.

For a model $m$ and a fixed hyperparameter configuration, the training problem is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{
\theta_m^*\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\theta\in\Theta_m}
\left\{
\frac{1}{|\mathcal D_{\mathrm{tr}}|}
\sum_{B\in\mathfrak B}\sum_{(u,v,y)\in B}
\ell\!\left(y,q_{\theta,B}^{(m)}(u,v)\right)
+\lambda\|\theta\|_2^2
\right\}.}
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}


\section{Baseline experiment}
\label{sec:experiment}
\paragraph{Objective.}
The experiment tests whether directional neighborhood aggregation improves recovery of missing ordered edges when orientation reflects latent vertex roles.
The skeleton, features and evaluation protocol are controlled to distinguish this effect from changes in graph size or model capacity.

\paragraph{Generating distribution.}
There are $n=180$ vertices with three equally sized roles $c_v\in\{0,1,2\}$ assigned by a random permutation.
For every unordered pair $\{u,v\}$, a skeleton edge is independently sampled with probability $q=0.15$.
Each sampled edge receives exactly one orientation, with
\begin{equation}
\Pr(u\to v\mid\{u,v\}\text{ sampled},c_u,c_v)
=\frac{1+\rho C_{c_u c_v}}2,\qquad
C=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&-1\\-1&0&1\\1&-1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
At $\rho=0$ orientation is random; at $\rho=1$ edges between different roles follow the cycle $0\to1\to2\to0$.
Within-role edges retain random orientation.
The same skeleton and orientation uniforms are reused across $\rho$ within each seed, so the total edge count is unchanged.
The expected number of edges is $q n(n-1)/2=2416.5$.
Features are $x_v=e_{c_v}+\varepsilon_v\in\mathbb R^6$, where $e_c$ is the coordinate vector for role $c$ among the first three coordinates and $\varepsilon_v\sim\mathcal N(0,I_6)$ independently.
Role indices are not supplied to the encoders.
Realized graphs contain 2324--2474 directed edges.

\paragraph{Models and settings.}
The experiment uses $\rho\in\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$ and seeds $0,\ldots,4$.
All encoders have two ReLU layers and produce 32-dimensional vertex embeddings.
Their common decoder is a $64\to32\to1$ MLP applied to ordered concatenations, with a ReLU hidden activation.
MLP, Sym and Dir use hidden width 32.
SymWide uses hidden width 48, matching Dir's total parameter count to within 1\% while retaining the same output dimension and decoder architecture.
Sym and Dir implement mean aggregation with separate self-feature transformations following GraphSAGE and Dir-GNN~\cite{sage,dirgnn}.

Adam uses learning rate $0.01$, $\lambda=10^{-4}$ and batches of at most 512 labeled pairs.
Training is limited to 120 epochs and stops after 25 epochs without an improvement in validation AP.
The best validation-AP checkpoint is selected; no hyperparameter search is performed.
The dyad split and query masking follow Section~\ref{sec:problem}.
Each model uses identical samples within each graph instance.
The implementation uses deterministic CPU operations with one thread.

\paragraph{Results.}
Table~\ref{tab:baseline} reports the maximum-direction setting; Figure~\ref{fig:orientation} includes every tested $\rho$.
At $\rho=1$, Dir improves over the capacity control by $0.148 \pm 0.020$ in ROC-AUC and $0.095 \pm 0.009$ in AP (paired means and sample standard deviations).
At weak directional structure, the directed encoder has no consistent advantage.
The main separation occurs at $\rho=1$, supporting the hypothesis for this generating family rather than a uniform benefit from direction-aware aggregation.
The results do not establish superiority on real-world graphs.
